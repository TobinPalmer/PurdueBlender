The Purdue Bell Tower: A Monument of Brick, Limestone, and Legacy

“One Brick Higher”

“On January 19, 1894, Purdue University dedicated Heavilon Hall, an engineering laboratory with a tower that soared above the modest skyline of the fledgling campus. Four days later, the structure lay in ruins, destroyed by explosion and fire. Amid the shock and tears of the disaster, Purdue President James Smart issued a rallying cry that crystallized the spirit of the University: ‘That tower shall go up one brick higher!’” A century later, that call would be answered. Dedicated in 1995, the Purdue Bell Tower is the spiritual and physical successor of the original Heavilon Hall. Rising in a brick clad steel frame, the Purdue Bell Tower stands as a 160 foot totemic representation of Purdue’s resolute spirit.

Today, it combines historical legacy with striking architectural presence. The Purdue Bell Tower was constructed for around 2.4 million dollars by the Scholer Corporation and Kettelhut Construction, Inc. It uses the four bells of the original Heavilon Hall and has a four-sided clockface. It has rings of string-coursed limestone accenting a steel skeleton clad in brick. Rising above the surrounding buildings, the Purdue Bell Tower serves as the symbolic heart of campus.

Location and Symbolism

The Purdue Bell Tower occupies a central and important location on the Purdue campus. It is situated near the south end of Centennial Mall, in front of the WALC and adjacent to Stanley Coulter and Wetherill. Its height, at 160 feet, is also relevant, as it means it can be seen from almost any point on campus. Its vertical emphasis, paired with its location, gives it a lighthouse effect by which anyone who sees it can be led to Purdue. Likewise, anyone who hears its bells chime likely knows they should go to class. It is this architecturally universalizing effect that has given the Purdue Bell Tower a kind of mythos. A product of this is the superstition that students who walk under it won’t graduate on time. Overall, it is indisputable that the bell tower is at the core of student experience, all the while not being a structure students interact with much.

Detail and Merit

As aforementioned, the Purdue Bell Tower was constructed using a steel frame clad in brick, accented by a limestone trim. The top of the tower is pyramidal, and is constructed of some kind of painted metal, likely aluminum. The four clock-faces are embedded into a cap of Indiana Limestone, a hallmark material of the state. Proceeding down the length of the tower are multiple string-coursed rings of limestone, leading down to an opening beneath the tower. It is in this opening that students never walk for fear of the curse, and it is also where the Purdue Bell Tower plaque is found. Finally, the bell tower features a computerized carillon that plays the Purdue Fight Song among other tunes.

Style and Consequence

The Purdue Bell Tower is more than just a structure, it is a story. A narrative that consolidates and bolsters the spirit of all who walk in its shadow. Its chimes carry across the campus, not just marking the hour but grounding us in something shared. For students, alumni, and anyone passing through, it’s a point of return, a part of the place that stays the same even as everything else keeps moving.

In the end, it’s not just about the bells or the bricks. It’s about what it means to belong to something bigger, to take pride in where you are and where you’re going. The tower doesn’t need to say much. It’s already said enough.
