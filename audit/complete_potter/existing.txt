BUILDING HISTORY AND CURRENT USE
The A.A. Potter Engineering Center (POTR) is a multi-purpose 
teaching and research facility administered by the Institute for 
Interdisciplinary Engineering Studies. The Center’s mission is to 
develop and promote innovative technologies in response to 
society’s needs. Its goal is to enhance quality of life.
The Center is named for Andrey Abraham Potter, the dean of engineering at Purdue from 1920 to 
1953. Dean Potter built Purdue into the largest and one of the most respected engineering colleges 
in the country. From 1945 to 1946, he served as acting president of Purdue; he was offered the pres­
idency but declined.
The Potter building is centrally located on the north campus, immediately to the east of the Wilmeth 
Active Learning Center (currently under construction). The building currently houses the Siegesmund 
Engineering Library, the Office of Professional Practice, Visual Analytics for Command, Control, and 
Interoperability Environments (VACCINE), Laboratory of Renewable Resources Engineering (LORRE), 
VLSI Design and Test Laboratory, the Center for Materials Under eXtreme Environment (CMUXE), the 
offices of the Division of Environmental and Ecological Engineering, and the CSOPS Research Entity 
for School Research. 
The building houses two named spaces. It is critical the following spaces be accommodated for and 
named in the newly renovated space.
K.S. FU ROOM FOR CROSS-DISCIPLINARY RESEARCH
The K.S. Fu Room for Cross-Disciplinary Research (Room 234) is a meeting room that comfortably 
seats up to 50 people and has been equipped for cross-disciplinary work, special research forums, 
site visits, cross-disciplinary seminars, presentations, workshops, roundtables, and the display of 
publications, posters, and audio/visual materials. 
This conference room was named in recognition of the late Professor King-Sun Fu because of his 
many accomplishments as Goss Distinguished Professor of Engineering. Professor Fu was a mem­
ber of the faculty of the Purdue School of Electrical Engineering from 1960 to 1985.
THE MALOTT COMMONS 
This common area honors Thomas J. Malott (BSME 1962, HDR 2002) and Sandra H. Malott, who 
donated in support of the Office of Professional Practice. The commons is an entity shared by the 
entire Potter Center.

ACCESSIBILITY
Though examination of accessibility to disabled persons is 
always important, it is especially important to this study because 
of the proposed relocation of the Disability Resource Center to 
the Potter Center. 
Because the DRC serves Purdue’s disabled student population, this building will be among the most 
heavily trafficked buildings on campus by students with special needs. While the entire building should 
be remodeled to be ADA compliant, areas specifically serving the DRC should be designed above 
and beyond code requirements to accommodate these students to the greatest extent possible.
As outlined in the following analysis, in its current condition, the Potter Center is not an accessible 
building. Therefore, it is the opinion of this study that the Center would not be fit for use by the DRC 
unless the DRC’s relocation occurred concurrently with accessibility upgrades.
PARKING
The Potter Center is not served by a designated parking lot. However, there are three parallel park­
ing spaces along the east side of the alley immediately east of Potter. These spaces are not in an 
ideal location close to an existing ramp to access the building; restriping and labeling these as ADA 
spaces appears to be the best available solution. Because the building is bound to the west and 
south by pedestrian-only roads, there is limited opportunity to create alternate parking solutions. 
A sidewalk should be constructed connecting these spaces to the sidewalks and entrance at the 
southeast entrance. There is currently no sidewalk along this side of the building.

BUILDING ENTRANCES AND ENTRANCE ACCESS
The finished floor level of the Potter Center is approximately 2' above grade level; therefore, the only 
accessible routes to the building are by ramp. Three ramps are present—one each at the northwest, 
northeast, and southeast corners. The ramp at the northwest corner is adjacent to the building’s 
loading dock and is primarily intended to function as a service ramp. The door at the end of this ramp 
leads toward the freight elevator, which is currently the building’s only elevator. 
The ramp near the southeast corner entrance is 106' from the southeast door. It is somewhat hidden 
behind a wall along the far west edge of the exterior terrace. A student in a wheelchair wishing to 
use the southwest entrance (the building’s main entrance) would need to travel between 138 and 
274' farther than an able-bodied peer. Because the south side of the building is along a pedestrian 
only path, the ramp itself is easily accessible.
ELEVATORS
The building currently contains a single freight elevator at the northwest corner of the building. 
Aesthetically, the freight elevator is not welcoming and is not a pleasure to use. On an emotional 
level, relegating wheelchair-bound students to ride with the building’s cargo is not hospitable. 
To examine a worst-case scenario, starting at a point at the southeast corner outside the building, 
an able-bodied student wishing to use the building’s main entrance and travel to room 205 at the 
southeast corner of the second floor would travel 186'. A wheelchair-bound peer would travel 727'. 
That is 541' (a tenth of a mile) farther to reach the same location. A new elevator, close to the build­
ing’s main entrance, would help eliminate this discrepancy.
RESTROOMS
No ADA-compliant restrooms exist in the Potter Center. Additionally, doors leading into restrooms 
do not have the necessary adjacent area to allow a wheelchair-bound student to operate the door. 
Restrooms on the second and third floor contain a stall with the width to accommodate ambulatory 
accessible use (38" wide), but the 4'-6" depth of these stalls is not compliant with the required 5' 
minimum.
Without significant restroom renovations, disabled students will likely need to leave the building 
to use the restroom. 
Recommended Accessibility Upgrades
In order for the Potter Center to house the Disability Resource Center, we strongly recommend ac­
cessibility upgrades be performed at a minimum on and around areas that would serve the DRC, but 
ideally throughout the building. The following upgrades should occur where applicable:
»» ADA-compliant door handles
»» ADA-compliant signage
»» ADA fire system notification
»» Proper spacing around doors
»» Proper clearance requirements in bathrooms
»» Proper door closure forces
»» Proper counter heights
»» Proper outlet and switch heights

ARCHITECTURAL ASSESSMENT
Though in respectable condition for an over 40-year-old structure, 
the Potter Center has had very few improvements or cosmetic 
upgrades over its lifespan and aesthetically appears quite dated. 
The building is generally designed in the “brutalist” or “brick brutalist” style popular for university, 
government, and high-rise housing projects from the 1950s to the mid-1970s. The façade is a com­
bination of large areas of unadorned, blank, brick and unbroken, repetitive facades of narrow 5'-4" 
precast concrete panels. The panels themselves feature very deep protrusions and offer quite a bit 
of depth to the façade. However, the continuous repetition of the panels tends to temper the visual 
interest created by their depth and instead lends the precast areas a sense of monotony. The build­
ing’s most visually intriguing exterior feature are its protruding brick stair towers with curved outer 
walls. 
On the interior, the building’s structural frame has the potential to be a highlighted feature. Its two-
way concrete beam system (essentially a large waffle slab) is rather attractive. The deep beams run­
ning in perpendicular directions give the ceiling a highly textured aesthetic. Unfortunately, the slabs 
are currently hidden behind dropped acoustic ceilings in most locations. The floor system, in addi­
tion to the generous floor-to-floor heights, creates a space with the potential to become an attractive 
interior. The terrazzo flooring along corridors on the first floor is also an asset. Terrazzo is a very 
tough and durable flooring material that can be refinished and restored to nearly as-new condition. 
With the exception of a row of seven large windows along the first story of the south face, window 
openings generally occur within the precast panels and are thus quite small at 5.4 sft each. The 
small window size, in conjunction with a 1'-4" precast concrete overhang directly above each win­
dow, allows only a limited amount of daylight into the building’s interior. This, in combination with 
very limited corridor glazing, results in an interior that lacks natural light. Replacing areas of precast 
panels with glazing and opening up corridor walls would certainly allow more daylight into areas of 
the building; however, achieving a desirable amount of daylight across the entire floorplate will be 
complicated by the building’s geometry. An “ideal” footprint for a well day-lit building is approximate­
ly 60' deep in the north-south direction and longer across the east-west direction. The Potter Center, 
however, is approximately a 160' square. Without cutting an internal atrium through the core of the 
building, it would be unreasonable to expect high levels of daylight to be present near the core, 
even after a substantial renovation. 
The dated interior and exterior appearance of the building is not likely to affect the DRC or Central 
Testing Center; however, one could argue it may be a hindrance to the objectives of the College 
of Engineering. The COE departments slated to move into this building are student-centric depart­
ments focused on recruiting students to the college and encouraging the next generation of engi­
neers. Engineering is a field of study focusing on innovation, research, and technology. This mes­
sage may be diluted when delivered to potential COE students in a facility that projects an image of 
being outdated.

STRUCTURAL ASSESSMENT
Based upon our review of existing drawings, we were able 
to ascertain information defining the structural systems for the 
building. 
During our walkthrough of the building, we visually verified to the greatest extent possible the ex­
isting structural systems. In general, the building structure appeared to be in good condition and 
generally as depicted in the original construction documents. In general, the structural system (both 
gravity resisting and lateral resisting) is intended to remain unchanged throughout the building as a 
result of the renovation of the facility.
FOUNDATION SYSTEM
The building has a full basement below its ground floor footprint. The basement is bounded by 
reinforced concrete walls supported on continuous reinforced concrete wall footings. The interior 
concrete columns are supported on a system of shallow reinforced concrete spread footings. The 
basement has a concrete slab on grade for its entire footprint.
FLOOR AND ROOF FRAMING SYSTEM
The floor and roof system is framed with a reinforced concrete waffle slab. The waffle slab is sup­
ported at regular intervals by reinforced concrete columns that continue down to the basement 
foundation level. At column locations, the concrete waffle slab construction transitions to solid slab.
Special framing exists in the southwest corner of the building from the foundation level up to the 
second-floor level. In this area, steel framing exists that serves as library stacks. The steel structure 
frames the first-floor level as well as an intermediate level that occurs between the basement level 
and the first-floor level. The concrete waffle slab structure that occurs typically throughout the build­
ing does not occur at the first-floor level in this location.
LATERAL SYSTEM
Although it could not specifically be identified on the existing plans nor definitely confirmed during 
the walkthrough, it can reasonably be assumed that the lateral force resisting system for the building 
is accomplished through reinforced concrete shear walls at each of the stair shafts in the building 
(four total). 
EXISTING MEZZANINE LEVEL
There is currently a steel stack framing system in the southwest corner of the building that partially 
vertically splits the 15’-4”-high basement level into two shorter 7’-8” floors. The headroom in these 
floor levels is approximately 7’-5”, with sprinkler heads at 7’-0”. These dimensions are lower than the 
7’-6” head height requirement permitted by code. Therefore, the mezzanine level and basement 
area under the mezzanine level cannot be utilized if left unchanged. Removing the mezzanine fram­
ing would allow the basement level underneath the mezzanine to be utilized; however, because the 
framing system also infills an opening on the first floor, the first-floor area would be lost and there 
would not be a net gain of area. By removing the mezzanine and infilling the 3,000-sft opening on 
the first floor, the project would gain the square footage on the basement level.

CIVIL ASSESSMENT
The building’s main entrance is currently located in the 
southeast corner of the building. This entrance’s elevation 
is substantially above the grade of the adjacent alley to the 
east as well as the current hardscape on the south side of the 
building. 
Access is gained to the main entrance from the surrounding lower grade by a series of stairs at the 
southeast corner of the building as well a ramp system that is located west of the stairs. There is 
currently no designated ADA-accessible parking areas that serve the building. 
To improve the access to the main entrance of the building, ADA-accessible parking should be con­
sidered near the main entrance of the building. This parking would preferably be located adjacent 
to the ramp system that leads to the main entrance or as parallel parking with a clearly defined ac­
cessible route to the ramp system. The feasibility of providing the ADA-accessible parking will need 
to be studied in further detail with the current improvements being constructed for the Centennial 
Mall Streetscape and Utility Tunnel Repair 2016 project. Analysis of the existing grades and available 
space along the existing alley to provide parallel parking should also be investigated to ensure the 
best accessible option is pursued.

CENTENNIAL MALL SITESCAPE AND UTILITY 
TUNNEL REPAIR
This site and landscaping project will occur immediately south 
of the Potter Center and around the Wilmeth Active Learning 
Center, immediately west of Potter. It will fully reimagine 
Centennial Mall. 
As a whole, the proposed design treats the Potter Center as a background building and is designed 
solely in deference to the Active Learning Center. Though this project directly affects the Potter 
Center’s “front yard,” it generally ignores the Potter building. There is some attention paid to install­
ing new landscaping at the southeast stair, which is currently the building’s main entrance. This is es­
pecially concerning with regard to the Disability Resource Center, a department whose sole purpose 
is to accommodate students with special needs, since the main entry stair is inaccessible. Students 
who need to use a ramp to access Potter from the south must travel to the west, to a ramp hidden in 
a small break in the landscaping. Students who cannot use the stairs are forced to travel a minimum 
of 138' and a maximum of 274' farther than their peers. 
It is the strong recommendation of this study that if the Potter Center is ultimately remodeled to 
create a new home for the Disability Resource Center, then accessibility to the building should be 
simultaneously addressed so all students can use a common entry path to the building. If the primary 
intent of the relocation of the DRC is to move it to a more accessible location, that goal will not be 
met without a concurrent exterior renovation.
The lack of integration of Potter’s circulation flow into the Centennial Mall project should be concern­
ing for the College of Engineering as well. The primary goal of the COE’s reimaging of Potter is to 
house all of the student-centered engineering departments under one roof. Many of these depart­
ments are focused on recruiting and encouraging the next generation of engineers—functions that 
will actively benefit from student foot traffic and a high degree of visibility to campus. These goals 
are not supported by the current Centennial Mall design.
