        A Look Around Purdue with Purdue University Press

        October 27, 2025| PurduePress

        As Purdue University Press continues to celebrate 65 years of scholarly publishing, let’s take a brief book tour around the Purdue University (West Lafayette) campus.

        Among many of our Founders series publications, this tour is heavily indebted all the way around to Ever True: 150 Years of Giant Leaps at Purdue University.

        The Beginning: John Purdue and University Hall

        University Hall, the centerpiece and only remaining building of the original six-building Purdue University campus, has recently undergone extensive renovation. John Purdue, the University’s founding benefactor, is buried in nearby Memorial Mall. A statue, placed within sight of University Hall, honors him.  Award-winning local journalist and Purdue University Press author John Norberg, has occasionally portrayed John Purdue for special events.

        Recommended Reading:

        The Midas of the Wabash: A Biography of John Purdue

        Uncle: My Journey with John Purdue

        Ever True

        200 Years of Tippecanoe County: A Community Tells Its Stories

        The Nobel Pursuit: Wetherill Hall and Brown Hall

        Richard Bendridge Wetherill Laboratory of Chemistry (WTHR), designated a National Historic Chemical Landmark in 2013, owes its name to Lafayette resident Richard Benbridge Wetherill. Wetherill was one of the first students in the initial Purdue Preparatory Class (a class for prospective students deemed unready for the university courses). He completed his degree elsewhere after one year at Purdue, but he would become a physician as well as the first president of the Tippecanoe County Historical Society (TCHA). The site for the Feast of the Hunter’s Moon was donated to the TCHA by Wetherill.  Eventually, he gave half a million dollars to Purdue for a chemistry building.

        Herbert C. Brown, the first current-at the time-member of the Purdue faculty to receive a Nobel Prize, held the Wetherill Distinguished Professorship Chair. Chemistry Building 2 (also known as Chemistry Building East, was renamed the Herbert C. Brown Laboratory of Chemistry (BRWN) in 1986.  In 1962 Ei-ichi Negishi attended a presentation by Herbert Brown (not yet a Laurate) and came to Purdue in 1963 to work as a postdoctoral researcher under Brown. In 1999 Negishi became the Herbert C. Brown Distinguished Professor of Chemistry, and, in 2010, he, too, was awarded a Nobel Prize. The day he was notified, students in his sophomore-level chemistry class were perplexed to find reporters and cameras in Wetherill Hall.

        Recommended Reading:

        Ever True

        200 Years of Tippecanoe County

        Remember the Ladies: Matthews Hall, Meredith Hall, and PMU

        Farmer, agricultural demonstrator, and educational speaker Virginia Claypool Meredith was instrumental in supporting William Latta (a professor of Agriculture at Purdue) in advocating for the education of women and a home economics program at Purdue. Her adopted daughter, Mary Matthews, later became the first dean of the School of Home Economics. The Home Economics Building was eventually renamed Matthews Hall in her honor, and is still in service after more than 100 years. For many years, Mary Matthews was the only female academic dean on campus, while her mother, Virginia Claypool Meredith, eventually served as the first female trustee at Purdue and played a key role in the design, construction, and financing of the Purdue Memorial Union. She (and her daughter) also aided President Elliott in researching and developing housing for students, and the Virginia C. Meredith Residence Hall was named in her honor.

        Recommended Reading:

        Purdue’s Female Founders: The Untold History of Trailblazing Women Faculty

        The Grand Old Man of Purdue University and Indiana Agriculture: A Biography of William Carol Latta

        The Queen of American Agriculture: A Biography of Virginia Claypool Meredith

        Divided Paths, Common Ground: The Story of Mary Matthews and Lella Gaddis, Pioneering Purdue Women Who Introduced Science into the Home

        Purdue Memorial Union: The First 100 Years 1924-2024

        Ever True

        Are You Ready for Some Football: Ross-Ade Stadium

        David Ross, a Lafayette-area local and a member of the Class of 1893, became an engineer and a very successful businessman with multiple companies and patents. Eventually, he became a full-time volunteer on behalf of Purdue University and an eventual alumni member of the board of trustees, even serving as president of the board. One of his first endeavors was to serve on an alumni committee raising funds for the Purdue Memorial Union. (He started by making his own contribution.) He is buried on land he donated to the University near what eventually became Slayter Center of Performing Arts.

        George Ade, another Purdue University graduate, was one of the most celebrated American writers of the early twentieth century with at least three productions on Broadway and one of the University’s most famous and popular graduates. He was the first alumnus elected to the Purdue University board of trustees, and editor of the Alumnus, in which he squared off against then-University President Stone (who also had a building named after him) about the importance of recreational activities for the student body.

        David Ross eventually recruited George Ade to help him plan, acquire the land for, and finance the Purdue University football stadium. The opposing team on dedication day was, of course, Indiana University. The two teams still compete for the Old Oaken Bucket.

        Recommended Reading:

        Ross-Ade: Their Purdue Stories, Stadium, and Legacies

        Ever True

        Cradle of Astronauts: Zucrow Labs, Grissom Hall, Chaffee Hall, and Amstrong Hall

        Maurice Zucrow, a man born before the Wright brothers’ first flight, was the first person to receive a Purdue Ph.D. from the newly organized Graduate School in 1928. After working in industry, Zucrow returned to the University to begin what became the world’s largest academic propulsion lab.

        Virgil “Gus” Grissom, Neil Armstong, Eugene Cernan, and Roger Chaffee attended Purdue University during Zucrow’s time. Grissom became the second American and third human launched into space, Armstrong became the first person to walk on the moon, and Cernan was the last Apollo astronaut on the lunar surface.

        Tragically, on Jan. 27, 1967, Grissom, Roger Chaffee, and fellow astronaut Ed White died at Kennedy Space Center when a flash fire consumed their spacecraft during a countdown demonstration test. Purdue responded to the tragedy by naming engineering buildings after Grissom and Chaffee. Neil Armstrong was among the attendees at the buildings’ 1968 dedication. Neil Armstrong Hall opened in 2007.

        Outside Armstrong Hall are the modeled footsteps from the lunar surface as well as a statue representing Neil Armstrong who remarked, “Only Purdue would put a statue of a scared freshman in front of an engineering building.”

        Cernan does not have a building named in his honor (yet).

        One of many later astronauts inspired by these four, Jerry Ross from Crown Point, Indiana, eventually launched on seven spaceflight missions.

        Recommended Reading:

        Ever True

        The Rocket Lab: Maurice Zucrow, Purdue University, and America’s Race to Space

        Wings of Their Dreams: Purdue in Flight, Third Edition

        Spacewalker: My Journey in Space and Faith as NASA’s Record-Setting Frequent Flyer

        Dear Neil Armstrong: Letters to the First Man from All Mankind and A Reluctant Icon: Letters to Neil Armstrong

        Calculated Risk: The Supersonic Life and Times of Gus Grissom, Revised and Expanded

        While this concludes today’s tour, there is much more around Purdue University campus to see, discover, learn, and read.
